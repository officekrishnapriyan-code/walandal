00 — SummaryThe paper in one page

India has built the taps. Whether the water is safe is still mostly assumed.

Since August 2019, the share of rural Indian homes with a tap has risen from 16.7% to 82.3%. The Jal Jeevan Mission’s promise is not only a tap: it is water of the quality set by IS 10500, supplied regularly and over the long term.[1] Quality, unlike a tap, cannot be inspected once and ticked off.

Water can change between the source and the glass, and most of all where it waits: in the overhead tanks that feed village pipe networks. Today it is checked by laboratory samples and field test kits. Those tests are essential, and their scale has grown fast. But each one is a snapshot, and between snapshots the quality of the water is assumed.

The evidence says the assumption often fails. In the Mission’s own 2024 assessment of villages that report a tap in every home, 24% of household water samples failed the microbiological test.[2] In its 2022 assessment, residual chlorine, the disinfectant that protects water on its way to the tap, was found within permissible limits in only 24% of household samples; 72% had none at all.[3]

This paper makes the case for monitoring a few operational parameters continuously at every village overhead tank: free residual chlorine, pH, turbidity and total dissolved solids. Each reading should be checked against IS 10500 as it arrives and shared with the people who run the water and the people who drink it. Sections 01–04 set out the evidence, sections 05–09 describe Aqua Insight, the system we are building to do this, and section 10 is plain about what sensors cannot do.

01 — ContextFrom access to assurance

In seven years, rural India went from one home in six with a tap to more than four in five.

When the Jal Jeevan Mission (Har Ghar Jal) began on 15 August 2019, 3.23 crore rural households, 16.7% of the total, had a tap connection. By 31 August 2026, the Mission’s information system recorded 15.94 crore of 19.36 crore rural households, or 82.34%.[7][4] That is about 12.7 crore more homes with a tap in seven years.

The promise includes quality

The Mission does not count a tap as enough. Its guidelines define a functional household tap connection as one that provides water “in adequate quantity, i.e. at least 55 lpcd, of prescribed quality, i.e. BIS:10500 standard, on regular basis, i.e. continuous supply in long-term”.[1]

Quantity and regularity can be checked by watching the supply. Quality is harder: it is invisible, it can change in hours, and it is measured today by taking samples to a laboratory or testing them with a kit. As coverage approaches every home, the question for the next phase moves from is there a tap? to is the water safe, today, at this tap?

“What cannot be measured cannot be managed. … it is necessary to have data on water supplied every day, the quality of water supplied as well as depth of water in bore wells.”

Jal Jeevan Mission, Operational Guidelines, December 2019, §11.1 [1]
02 — The problemWhere quality changes

Water that leaves the source safe does not always arrive safe.

In a typical village scheme, water is pumped from a borewell, well or bulk supply into an overhead tank, and flows from there by gravity through the village pipes to each home. The Mission’s guidelines list the overhead tank as part of the in-village infrastructure, and ask for the supply from each one to be measured with sensors.[1] In the Mission’s 2020–21 functionality survey, 55.5% of villages reported an overhead tank in their piped water scheme.[9]

Between the treatment point and the tap, three things can go wrong, and each leaves a trace in the water. WHO notes that outbreaks in distribution systems “have been linked to cross-connections, contamination during storage, low water pressure and intermittent supply”.[10]

  • The disinfectant runs out. Chlorine is added to kill germs, and enough of it has to survive all the way to the tap. WHO advises that “a chlorine residual should be maintained throughout the distribution system” and that free chlorine at the point of delivery should be at least 0.2 mg/l.[10] The government’s 2024 water supply manual is blunt: “absence of residual chlorine could indicate potential presence of heavy contamination.”[11]
  • Stored water deteriorates. Water quality in a reservoir “may also deteriorate due to excessively prolonged periods of stagnant conditions”.[11] Unprotected tanks “are at risk of faecal contamination from birds and vermin”.[12] A February 2026 advisory for urban water systems asks for reservoirs and overhead tanks to be cleaned “at least twice a year”.[13]
  • Intermittent supply lets dirt in. When pipes run empty between supply hours, pressure drops and contaminated water can be drawn in through joints and cracks. “It is now well established that intermittent water supply leads to health risks for users,” the same manual states.[14] WHO calls intermittent supplies “frequently associated with contamination”.[10]

The source itself is not always safe

Many rural schemes draw on groundwater, and in many districts it carries natural contaminants that no amount of storage management will remove. In the Central Ground Water Board’s 2024 sampling, one in five samples exceeded the BIS limit for nitrate, and one in twelve exceeded it for fluoride.[16]

These contaminants need laboratory tests and, where they are found, treatment or a different source. Continuous sensors at the tank do not detect them (section 10). As reported by States, access to potable water has been ensured in every arsenic- and fluoride-affected habitation; in January 2025 the last 568 of them were served by interim community water purification plants.[17]

03 — Why it mattersWhat is at stake

Unsafe water is still one of India’s largest preventable causes of illness and death.

WHO’s latest burden-of-disease estimate, for 2019, attributes 4,97,738 deaths in India to unsafe drinking water, sanitation and hygiene. That is 36.4 deaths per 1,00,000 people. Unsafe drinking water alone accounted for 2,03,863 deaths from diarrhoea.[5] Worldwide, at least 1.7 billion people used a drinking-water source contaminated with faeces in 2022.[18]

Routine reporting shows the illness behind those deaths. In 2022, States reported nearly 56 lakh cases of acute diarrhoeal disease and more than 12.3 lakh cases of typhoid (provisional figures).[19] In the National Family Health Survey of 2019–21, 7.3% of children under five had had diarrhoea in the two weeks before the survey, down from 9.2% in 2015–16.[20]

Measuring quality is what made India’s progress visible

The WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP) counts drinking water as “safely managed” only when it comes from an improved source that is on premises, available when needed and free from faecal and priority chemical contamination.[21] Until 2025 it had no national estimate for India. Its 2025 report was the first, “due to newly compiled data on drinking-water quality in urban areas”.[6] Water-quality data is what made the national figure possible.

Even on the JMP’s measure, about one rural Indian in eight in 2024 did not have drinking water from an improved source free from contamination. Tests at the tap tell a sharper story: in the Mission’s 2024 assessment of Har Ghar Jal villages, 24% of household samples failed the microbiological test.[2]

04 — The gapBetween tests

India tests more water than ever. Each test is still a snapshot.

The Mission has built a very large water-testing network. In January 2025 there were 2,162 water-testing laboratories, 1,572 of them accredited or recognised by NABL.[17] In 2025–26, up to 21 October 2025, 2,843 laboratories tested 38.78 lakh samples.[22] In 2024–25, up to 15 January 2025, laboratories tested 62 lakh samples and found 3.18 lakh contaminated, and 83 lakh more were tested with field test kits. 24.8 lakh women have been trained to use the kits.[17]

The testing schedule is set by the Mission’s 2021 framework. Laboratories test every drinking-water source “at least twice a year, i.e., before and after the monsoon for bacteriological parameters and at least once in a year for chemical parameters”. In every village, five trained people, preferably women, test the sources and two tail-end taps with field test kits at least once a month.[15] The framework itself notes that a kit “gives an indicative result”.

The Mission’s own surveys show what a monthly rhythm can miss. In 2022, 87% of households received water that passed laboratory tests. Yet residual chlorine was within permissible limits in only 24% of household samples, and only 21% of villages had a mechanism for chlorination.[3]

What a snapshot misses

A sample shows the water at one moment. Free chlorine can drop quickly: when contamination gets in, when water stands too long in a tank, or when supply is interrupted. WHO notes that “a sudden disappearance of an otherwise stable residual can indicate ingress of contamination”.[10] If the drop comes and goes between two samples, a household can drink unprotected water for days without any test recording it.

The official direction is already set

None of this is a new idea. The Mission’s 2019 guidelines ask for the supply “from each ESR [elevated storage reservoir] to be measured using modern sensors based IoT solutions”, and for public health engineering departments to “employ water quality sensors and IoT based monitoring systems”.[1] Its 2021 framework calls for “technological innovations such as sensor-based IoTs which provide automatic data on water supply quantity, quality and regularity”, starting with pilot projects to be “demonstrated, and later be planned for scaling it up”.[15]

The government’s 2024 operations manual goes further: “Wherever online water quality parameters are monitored, the laboratory water quality test may not be required for such parameters.”[11] WHO explains why speed matters: laboratory tests for germs “are often of limited use for operational monitoring, because the time taken to process and analyse water samples does not allow operational adjustments to be made prior to supply”.[10]

The opportunity

Laboratories and field-test kits tell us what is in the water. Continuous sensors at the tank tell us when it changed. Together they turn periodic checks into a record that operators, officials and residents can act on, and trust.

05 — Our approachAqua Insight

One node on every tank, one shared view of the water.

Aqua Insight is the drinking-water system Team WAL & AL is building for village overhead tanks. A solar-powered node on the tank measures the water around the clock. Every reading is checked against IS 10500 as it arrives, and the result reaches the people who run the tank, the officials who oversee it and the residents who drink from it.

What the node measures

The node measures four parameters continuously: free residual chlorine, pH, turbidity and total dissolved solids (TDS). Water level is planned as a fifth reading, so the same view shows supply as well as quality. We chose these four for two reasons. They can be measured reliably by sensors that sit in the water. And between laboratory tests, they answer the questions that matter most: is the water still disinfected, has something disturbed or entered it, and has its mineral content changed?

What it does with each reading

  1. Checks it against IS 10500 as it arrives, and flags a breach in real time.
  2. Warns before a limit is crossed. A trend fitted to recent readings projects the next few hours, so a falling chlorine level raises a warning while there is still time to act.
  3. Alerts the right person. Alerts go to the people responsible for that tank, who acknowledge and resolve them in the app. Each action is recorded, so it is clear who acted and when.
  4. Scores the water. A 0–100 Water Quality Index for every tank, village and district (section 07).
  5. Watches its own sensors. It flags readings that flat-line and calibration that is due, so a faulty sensor is not mistaken for safe water.
  6. Closes the loop. Rules can turn readings into commands sent back to the device, within limits that operators set.
  7. Reports compliance. IS 10500 reports by village, exported as PDF.
  8. Answers the resident’s question. An “Is the water safe?” card gives a plain answer, checked against IS 10500.
06 — How it worksSense · Connect · Understand · Act

From the tank to a decision, in one loop.

Aqua Insight runs on the same four-step method behind every WAL & AL system. We build and run every layer ourselves, from the node’s firmware to the apps, so the system behaves as one product and a problem in any layer is ours to fix.

Design choices

  • Built for the tank, not the lab. The node runs on solar power and a battery, so it does not depend on mains power at the tank.
  • Hosted in India. Readings, accounts and logs are kept in Indian data centres.
  • One app for every role, on phone and web. Owners, operators and officials get the same features on both, from one codebase, so field staff and offices see the same thing.
  • Nothing lost in transit. Readings pass through a durable queue before they are processed, so a burst of traffic or a slow step does not drop data.
07 — MethodWater Quality Index

One score from 0 to 100, without hiding a breach.

Four readings per tank, across hundreds of tanks, are too many numbers for a busy official. The Aqua Insight Water Quality Index (WQI) turns each tank’s readings into one score from 0 to 100, which also averages up to a village and a district.

  1. Score each parameter. Inside its safe band, a parameter scores 85–100, highest near the middle of the band. For turbidity and TDS, lower is better, so low values are not penalised. Across the caution band just beyond the limit, the score falls from 85 to 50. Beyond that, it falls from 50 towards 0.
  2. Weight the scores. The weights favour the parameters most directly tied to health: free residual chlorine 35%, pH 25%, turbidity 25% and TDS 15% (Fig. 8).
  3. Grade the result. 90 and above is Excellent, 75–89 Good, 50–74 Fair, and below 50 Poor.
The index never overrides a limit

The WQI summarises; it does not decide. Each reading is still checked against its own IS 10500 limit, and a single parameter outside its limit raises its own alert, whatever the overall score.

The bands and weights in version 1.0 are a starting point. We will check them against laboratory results in field trials and record any change in later versions of this paper.

08 — PeopleOne view, every role

The same live record for everyone responsible for the water.

Village water is everyone’s business and often no one’s job. Aqua Insight gives each person a view scoped to what they are responsible for, built on one shared record. A problem is then visible to the people who can fix it and to the people who oversee them.

09 — TrustSecurity, privacy and standards

A system that vouches for water has to be trustworthy itself.

If the data can be faked, lost or leaked, the “safe” card means nothing. Aqua Insight is designed to the Indian standards and rules that apply to drinking water, personal data and cyber-security, and to the leading open standard for mobile app security.

Security controls, by design

These controls are part of Aqua Insight’s design, and an independent audit will test them before launch.

  • Devices. Each node has its own identity and may send only its own readings. Commands to devices are checked on the server, against the sender’s role and the tank’s limits.
  • Data. Encrypted in transit and at rest. Data and logs are kept in India.
  • Access. The same role and tank-scope checks run on every request and every device command, not only in the app’s screens. Officials and administrators use multi-factor sign-in.
  • Apps. Short-lived sessions in encrypted storage on the device, lockout after repeated failed sign-ins, an optional biometric lock, and no keys or credentials inside the app.
  • Assurance. An independent vulnerability assessment and penetration test by a CERT-In empanelled auditor before launch, then every year.
10 — LimitsWhat sensors don’t replace

Continuous sensors are not a laboratory.

We think it matters to say clearly what continuous monitoring does not do.

  • They do not detect germs directly. IS 10500 requires that E. coli “shall not be detectable in any 100 ml sample”,[23] and only a microbiological test can show that. Free chlorine, pH and turbidity show whether the conditions for safe water hold. WHO describes chlorine residual monitoring as giving “a rapid indication of problems that will direct measurement of microbial parameters”.[10] It tells you when to test; it does not replace the test.
  • They do not cover every contaminant. Arsenic, fluoride, nitrate, iron, uranium and many other contaminants need laboratory tests (section 02). TDS is not a stand-in for any of them.
  • They need care. Sensors in water drift and foul, and must be cleaned and calibrated on schedule. Aqua Insight flags calibration that is due and readings that flat-line, but someone still has to do the work.
  • The tank is not every tap. The node measures the water in the tank. Pipes downstream can still let dirt in, especially when supply is intermittent, and IS 10500 tests free chlorine “at consumer end”.[23] Testing at tail-end taps stays essential.
What continuous monitoring adds

Timing. It shows when something changed, where, and for how long. Laboratory and field-kit tests can then be sent where they are needed, and a problem can be found within hours rather than at the next scheduled sample.

11 — StatusRoadmap

Where Aqua Insight is today, and what comes next.

Aqua Insight is in development. The node, the cloud platform and the apps are being built and tested together against a simulated tank, ahead of field trials. Nothing in this paper describes a deployed system or reports results from one.

We are looking for partners for phases 2 and 3: gram panchayats and village water committees, district and state water departments, NGOs and CSR programmes, and research labs that can run laboratory tests alongside the sensors.

12 — ConclusionMeasured, not assumed

India has built the taps. The next task is knowing the water is safe, around the clock.

The Jal Jeevan Mission has changed what rural India can expect from its water supply. The next step is to make sure that what flows from the taps meets the standard the Mission itself set: water of prescribed quality, supplied regularly, over the long term.

Laboratories and field test kits will remain the reference for what is in the water. Continuous monitoring at the overhead tank adds what they cannot: a record of when the water changed, shared at once with the people who can act on it. The Mission’s own guidelines already ask for sensors. What remains is to prove them in the field, at a cost and an upkeep that villages can sustain.

That is the work Aqua Insight is built for. We would like to do it with the people who run, oversee and drink village water.
